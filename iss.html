International Space Station

The ISS is a continuously crewed research laboratory in low Earth orbit. Learn the basics and find official resources.

What is the ISS?

The International Space Station is a multinational orbital laboratory used for scientific research, technology demonstrations, Earth observation and long-duration human spaceflight.

Because it orbits relatively close to Earth, the station can sometimes be seen as a bright moving object shortly after sunset or before sunrise.

Why does the ISS look like a moving star?

The station reflects sunlight toward observers on Earth. Its apparent brightness changes with geometry and viewing conditions.

When can I see it?

Visibility depends on your location and the station's orbit. Check an official tracking service for local opportunities.

How the ISS stays in orbit

The International Space Station travels around Earth in low Earth orbit. Gravity provides the inward acceleration needed for orbital motion, while the station's forward velocity carries it around the planet. The atmosphere is extremely thin at its altitude but still produces a small amount of drag, so orbital maintenance is periodically required.

What happens aboard the station?

The ISS supports experiments in microgravity, technology demonstrations, Earth observation and long-duration human spaceflight. Researchers can study processes that behave differently when the constant pull of gravity is greatly reduced.

Why viewing opportunities are short

A visible pass is usually brief because the station moves quickly across the sky. It is easiest to see when the station is illuminated by the Sun while the observer's local sky is relatively dark. Clouds, haze, city lights and the station's position relative to the horizon all affect the experience.

Where to get current information

SpaceFleet provides background information rather than claiming a live ISS position. For current sighting opportunities, use an established tracking service and enter your observation location.
