How Space Missions Work

A mission is more than a launch. Every spacecraft moves through a sequence of design, testing and operational phases shaped by its objective and destination.

Start with the mission objective

Mission teams begin by defining what they need to measure, demonstrate, deliver or explore. The objective drives the spacecraft’s instruments, power system, communications, trajectory and operating environment.

A weather satellite, planetary probe and crewed laboratory can all be called space missions, but their engineering priorities are very different.

Design and testing

Engineers translate the mission objective into hardware and software requirements. Spacecraft are then tested for vibration, thermal conditions, vacuum, electromagnetic compatibility and other stresses associated with launch and the space environment.

Testing is not only about proving that a spacecraft works. It is also about discovering weaknesses while there is still an opportunity to correct them on Earth.

Launch and early operations

Launch places the spacecraft on its initial trajectory. After separation from the launch vehicle, teams perform early operations such as establishing communications, deploying solar arrays or antennas and checking the health of major systems.

This phase can be especially busy because the spacecraft transitions from a controlled ground environment to autonomous operation in space.

Cruise, insertion and commissioning

For missions traveling beyond low Earth orbit, the spacecraft may spend months or years in cruise. Navigation updates and trajectory maneuvers can be required along the way.

When the spacecraft reaches its destination, it may need to enter orbit, land, fly by a target or deploy a payload. Commissioning then checks instruments and systems before full science operations begin.

Science and operations

During routine operations, mission teams collect data, schedule observations, manage spacecraft resources and respond to anomalies. Some missions are designed for a specific operational lifetime; others can continue if their systems remain healthy and useful.

Public mission pages can contain schedules, status updates, images and scientific results. Because those details change, current mission status should always be checked with the operator.

How to research a mission

  • Identify the mission operator or space agency.
  • Find the official mission page.
  • Check the publication or update date.
  • Look for primary technical or science sources when a claim is important.
  • Use established space publications for additional context rather than treating a secondary article as the only source.
