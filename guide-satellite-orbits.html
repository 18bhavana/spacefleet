ORBITAL BASICS

LEO, MEO and GEO Explained

A practical introduction to the three commonly discussed Earth-orbit regimes and the trade-offs behind them.

What an orbit actually is

An orbit is a continuously curved path produced by the combination of an object’s velocity and the gravity of the body it is orbiting. A spacecraft is not simply “floating” above Earth: it is continually falling toward Earth while moving sideways fast enough that the ground curves away beneath it.

Altitude changes the size and timing of an orbit. Lower orbits generally have shorter periods, while higher circular orbits take longer to complete. Orbital inclination also matters because it determines which latitudes the spacecraft can pass over.

Low Earth orbit (LEO)

LEO is the region commonly used for spacecraft that need relatively close access to Earth. Earth-observation satellites, crewed spacecraft and many communications constellations operate in low orbits because the lower altitude can support detailed observation and relatively low communication latency.

LEO spacecraft move across the sky quickly for a ground observer. Atmospheric drag, although the atmosphere is very thin at these altitudes, can gradually change an orbit. Operators therefore use updated orbital information and, when necessary, perform orbit-maintenance maneuvers.

Medium Earth orbit (MEO)

MEO sits above LEO and below geostationary orbit. Navigation satellite systems are a well-known use because their orbital altitude provides broad coverage while allowing a constellation of satellites to provide positioning and timing services.

From the ground, MEO satellites generally move more slowly across the sky than LEO satellites. Their longer orbital periods and larger coverage footprints are useful for missions that need persistent regional or global service from a constellation.

Geostationary orbit (GEO)

A geostationary orbit is a special circular equatorial orbit with an orbital period matching Earth’s rotation. A satellite in the correct orbit appears nearly fixed above one longitude to an observer on Earth, which is useful for communications and weather-monitoring applications.

GEO spacecraft are much farther from Earth than LEO spacecraft. That distance changes communication delay, launch requirements and the apparent size of the area a satellite can cover. The orbit is also a limited resource because satellites must be carefully positioned to avoid interference and maintain safe separation.

Why mission designers choose different orbits

  • Earth observation missions often value proximity and repeat coverage.
  • Communications missions may value wide coverage or specific geographic service areas.
  • Navigation systems depend on constellation geometry and timing.
  • Scientific missions choose orbits based on the measurement environment they need.

No orbit is universally “best.” The appropriate orbit is a mission-design decision involving coverage, resolution, communications, radiation, launch energy, lifetime, station keeping and operational cost.

What this means for satellite tracking

When you open a tracking service, orbit type helps explain what you see. A LEO satellite can cross the visible sky in minutes, while a higher-orbit spacecraft changes apparent position more slowly. A geostationary satellite can appear almost stationary even though it is moving rapidly around Earth.

For current positions and pass predictions, use a specialist tracker with current orbital elements. SpaceFleet provides the concepts; the live service should be treated as the source for the current prediction.
